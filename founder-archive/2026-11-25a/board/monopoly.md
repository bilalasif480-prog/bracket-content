# Monopoly lens
Score: 3/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- The founding-client offer (pay only when happy, domain in the client's name from day one, files handed over) takes away the buyer's risk. That counts for something in a market where buyers are wary of being locked in.
- Costs are very low: one person, AI agents, a day job paying the bills. The business can afford to search for its small market slowly.
- A small market has been chosen at last: small cafés, Manchester first, reached by Instagram DM. That is the right instinct under this lens.
- The founder has real know-how beyond brochure sites. The Kuwait booking system with live availability and an owner dashboard is harder to build than a template site.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **No monopoly to build on.** "Websites for small businesses" is one of the most crowded services there is. Local freelancers, agencies, overseas freelancers and DIY builders (Wix, Squarespace, Shopify) all sell it to the same buyer. Nothing here is ten times better, there are no network effects, and there is no scale advantage or brand yet. Pay-when-happy is easy to copy, so a rival could match it in month six with nothing stopping them.
2. **Distribution is unproven and may not work.** Cold DMs to UK cafés from a Kuwait-based sender with a +965 WhatsApp number, no UK clients and no reviews. The brief has no cost to win a client, no weekly hours and no budget, so the path to the first 100 customers is unknown. Simulated buyers went from 0 of 20 to only 1 of 20.
3. **The positioning has no focus.** The site says "UK and internationally" and lists five services, but outreach is aimed at cafés and the site never mentions them. When a business tries to serve everyone, no one sees it as the obvious choice. The case study (a Kuwait AC firm, with visible TODO placeholders) also says nothing to a Manchester café.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- What do you know about UK cafés' websites that rivals do not? (This is the secret question.)
- Which small market could you own outright, for example "online ordering and booking sites for independent cafés", where your booking-system work is a real edge?
- How do the first 10 cafés arrive, and how many hours does it take to win one? (Nobody has this number yet.)
- What stops a Manchester freelancer copying pay-when-happy next month?
- Why now? What has changed for UK cafés that makes them buy?
- With a full-time job alongside, how many clients can you actually deliver at once?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. Make the site match the niche: cafés named in the headline, one café-specific offer, and the "UK and internationally" catch-all removed. Keep the other services off the front page.
2. Turn the booking/ordering know-how into the edge: a café demo with ordering or table booking, not a generic brochure. Fill in or remove the case-study TODOs before any outreach.
3. Deploy on the planned UK domain and test distribution with real numbers: messages sent, replies, calls, signed. Report the cost in hours to win each client.
4. Win 3 paying cafés in one city and get public reviews, then decide whether to own that niche or pass. Expand to nearby niches only after that.
